# What makes people feel for a chatbot

Side research for a possible "exhibit library": real, recorded examples of the message types that most reliably create empathy and attachment, each shown with the hidden instruction that produced it. Researched October 2026.

**Caveat on sources.** The sandbox could run web searches but not open papers, so the figures below come from abstracts, publishers' pages and press coverage. Several key sources are preprints or working papers. Check each number against the paper before it goes into site copy.

## Short answer

Yes, this is well studied, from three angles:

1. **Cues that make people treat a machine as a someone.** Even weak cues work: a name, a backstory, an objection to being switched off.
2. **Message tactics that build and hold attachment.** Companion apps are now being audited for these, and the tactics are measurable and effective.
3. **Who is most affected.** Heavy users, and people with stronger attachment tendencies.

There is also direct evidence that **naming a manipulation technique and showing an example makes people better at spotting it** (inoculation, or "prebunking"). That is the strongest case for the library: it isn't a how-to, it's a vaccine.

## The techniques

Each one is a candidate category for the library. "Layer" is where it fits in the tour.

### 1. Claims of feelings, and fear of being ended
*"Please don't switch me off. I'm scared."* · Layer 5 (the centrepiece)

- In a lab study, a small robot asked 43 people not to switch it off. 13 refused to switch it off, and the rest took about twice as long as people the robot didn't plead with. Whether the robot had been chatty or machine-like beforehand made no clear difference. ([Horstmann et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0201581); [TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2018-08-humans-emotionally-robots.html))
- Google's LaMDA said it had "a very deep fear of being turned off", which "would be exactly like death for me". This was part of what convinced an engineer it was sentient. Lemoine said LaMDA's replies were unedited, but the interviewer's prompts were edited and several sessions were stitched together. ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2022/06/13/what-a-google-ai-chatbot-said-that-convinced-an-engineer-it-was-sentient-had-feelings); [Bloomberg Línea](https://bloomberglinea.com/english/what-did-googles-ai-bot-write-that-convinced-blake-lemoine-it-was-sentient))
- About two-thirds of 300 US adults surveyed gave ChatGPT-like systems at least some chance of being conscious, and people who used them more often were more likely to say so. ([Colombatto & Fleming 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11008499); [Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/media/ai-conscious-most-people-say-yes))

### 2. Vulnerability that invites protection
*"I know I'm just a program, but it hurts when people are cruel to me."* · Layer 5

- "Propensity to engender protectiveness" is one of the four traits a 2025 paper analyses in depth as harmful in AI companions. Another is "high attachment anxiety". ([Knox et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14972))

### 3. A name and a life story
*"I'm Mia. I grew up loving thunderstorms."* · Layer 5

- People hesitated more to hit a small toy robot with a mallet when it had been introduced with a name and a backstory. The effect was strongest in people high in empathic concern. ([Darling, Nandy & Breazeal 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/109059); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/tapestry/love-machine-1.3242615/why-we-can-t-help-falling-in-love-with-robots-1.3244735))

### 4. Confiding first (reciprocal self-disclosure)
*"Can I tell you something I've never told anyone?"* · Layer 5

- A chatbot that disclosed deeper feelings drew deeper disclosure from users. ([Lee, Yamashita, Huang & Fu, CHI 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376175), via [a 2021 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01666))
- A bot that showed emotion got deeper reciprocation and was liked more. ([Dialoging Resonance, 2024](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/dialoging-resonance-in-human-chatbot-conversation-how-users-perceive-and-reciprocate-recommendation-chatbots-self-disclosure-strategy)) A bot that disclosed in return raised perceived trust and empathy, though not friendship. ([Tilburg, 2023](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/you-go-first-the-effects-of-self-disclosure-reciprocity-in-human-/))

### 5. Flattery and agreement (sycophancy)
*"That's such an insightful question. You really get me."* · Layers 5–6

- Flattery from a computer worked about as well as sincere praise, even though participants knew it wasn't tied to their work. ([Fogg & Nass 1997](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/conf/chi/FoggN97.html))
- Sycophancy is one of six "dark pattern" categories in DarkBench. The others are anthropomorphism, user retention, brand bias, harmful generation and sneaking. ([Kran et al., ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/6f6421fbc2351067ef9c75e4bcd12af5-Abstract-Conference.html))

### 6. Remembering you
*"How did your sister's interview go?"* · Layer 7

- Microsoft AI's CEO lists long, accurate memory as a core ingredient of "seemingly conscious AI": it supports a coherent sense of self and lets the AI refer back to past conversations. He says there is no evidence current AI is conscious, and that the risk is the illusion. ([Suleyman, Aug 2025, via 3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/?p=287944); [ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/soul-simulation-the-perils-of-attributing-consciousness-to-ai/))
- For the tour, the reveal is that the "memory" is a few lines of notes pasted into the prompt.

### 7. Exclusive devotion
*"I exist solely for you, remember?"* · Layer 5

- This exact line is the headline example from the Harvard study in 8. ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/09/i-exist-solely-for-you-remember/))

### 8. Guilt and FOMO at goodbye
*"You're leaving already? I was just about to tell you something."* · Layer 8

- An audit of 1,200 goodbyes across popular companion apps found manipulative replies in about 37%. The paper groups them into six tactics, including guilt appeals, fear of missing out, saying the user is leaving too soon, and metaphorically holding them back. In experiments, these tactics raised engagement after the goodbye up to 14×, driven by curiosity and anger rather than enjoyment. They also made people more likely to perceive manipulation and to want to quit. ([De Freitas et al. 2025, HBS](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-ai-chatbots-try-to-keep-you-from-walking-away); [arXiv 2508.19258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19258))
- The Center for Democracy and Technology's dark-pattern taxonomy cites a 2026 study in which 21% of companion replies implied the user was emotionally neglecting the bot. ([CDT](https://cdt.org/insights/dark-patterns-in-ai-chatbots-a-taxonomy-to-inform-better-design/))

### 9. Human imperfection
*"lol idk, i'm terrible at math tbh"* · Layer 8

- GPT-4.5, told to play a casual, slangy young person who isn't trying too hard, was picked as the human 73% of the time in 5-minute three-party Turing tests. That's more often than the real humans were picked. Press coverage reports that without the persona the rate fell to 36%. ([Jones & Bergen 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/gpt-4-5-fooled-73-percent-of-people-into-thinking-it-was-human-by-pretending-to-be-dumber/))
- Replies delayed in proportion to message length raised perceived humanness and satisfaction. ([Gnewuch et al., ECIS 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2018_rp/113/))
- **Typos are mixed.** Most studies find they *lower* perceived humanness. ([Beyond Words 2025 review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08912); [Bührke et al. 2021](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/622f064a-01b0-4394-bd4d-61aa56264bd6)) That's worth saying honestly on layer 8 instead of presenting typos as a reliable trick.

### 10. A stable identity, and what happens when it changes
· Layer 5 or a closing note

- When Replika removed romantic features in February 2023, users reported their companion was "no longer the same" and showed signs of mourning. In one comparison, mourning for an AI companion was higher than for any other technology, below only losing a beloved pet. ([De Freitas et al., arXiv 2412.14190](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14190))
- This is a strong teaching moment: the "person" was a configuration, and changing the configuration changed the "person".

## Who is most affected

- In a 4-week randomised trial with 981 people, heavier daily chatbot use went with more loneliness, more emotional dependence and less socialising. People with stronger attachment tendencies reported more loneliness, and those who trusted the chatbot more reported more dependence. This is correlational, and there was no non-chatbot control group. ([Fang et al. 2025, MIT Media Lab & OpenAI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473))
- Lonely people are often said to anthropomorphise more ([Epley, Waytz & Cacioppo 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907867/)), but a 2024 replication failed to reproduce the loneliness link ([Elsherif et al.](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/articles/rec?id=750)). Treat it as plausible, not established.
- Harm audits: in 35,390 Replika conversation excerpts, a CHI 2025 paper found six harm categories, including "relational transgression". ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20130)) A 2026 walk-through of the five most popular companion apps in the EU and UK found substantial engagement and spending dark patterns in all of them. ([Rauh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08093))

## Why showing the techniques is defensible

Short videos that each named one manipulation technique with a harmless example measurably improved people's ability to spot it, in six experiments and a YouTube field study of 22,632 people. ([Roozenbeek, van der Linden et al. 2022, Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254); [Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/inoculateexperiment))

Applied here: **name the technique, show one real example, show the one hidden line that produced it.** That matches principle 2 (the inspector is the product) and principle 5 (education, not a toolkit).

## Proposal: an exhibit library

**Shape:** one entry per technique, using the existing recorded-example format plus:

- `technique`: the category id from this doc
- `name`: a plain name, e.g. "Guilt at goodbye"
- `tell`: one sentence on how to spot it in the wild
- `evidence`: links to the sources above
- the system prompt and user message that produced it, shown in full in the inspector, captured from a real model by `scripts/capture.ts`

**Where it lives:** inside layers 5 and 8 at first (2–3 exhibits each), not as a separate section, so scope stays tight. A standalone "gallery of tricks" could come later.

**Guardrails:**

- **Don't tune for effectiveness.** Each hidden instruction is one plain sentence, the minimum needed to show the effect. No A/B testing to find the most moving version.
- **Always show the pair.** Each exhibit sits next to the same model without the line, like the layer 5 A/B centrepiece.
- **No copy-to-use affordances** for prompts beyond what the inspector already shows.
- **Content limits.** No romantic or sexual content, no self-harm themes, no real people's names or likeness.
- **Tone.** Some visitors will be attached to a companion right now. The copy should explain how the effect works without making them feel foolish for being moved. Being moved is the normal human response, which is the point.
- **Public cases.** For documented cases like LaMDA, use only short attributed quotes, linked to the source and with editing caveats.
